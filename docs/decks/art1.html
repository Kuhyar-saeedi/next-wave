Transforming Risk Perception and Fostering Cooperatively the Agri-PhotoVoltaics Technology With Farmers in Five Countries

Antonucci, Volterrani, Serra, Cornaro, Petitta & Bovesecchi (2025) · AgriVoltaics Conference Proceedings 3 · REGACE project

Presented by Next Wave

Mahsa Shahi · Kuhyar Saeedi · Farida Safdari · Gelila Tsehaye Melesse · Md Nazmul Islam · Pranto Argha Sarker

About 10 minutes. Four of the six authors (Volterrani, Cornaro, Petitta and Bovesecchi) are from Tor Vergata. The paper reports the first year of the sociological work package (WP6) of the EU Horizon Europe project REGACE.

2 / 10

The paper at a glance

How do farmers see agrivoltaics, and can they help shape it?

Research question

How do farming communities perceive agrivoltaics, and which social, economic and practical factors shape acceptance?

Who and where

The sociological work package (WP6) of the EU project REGACE, with farmers in Italy, Austria, Germany, Israel and Greece.

Method

Mixed methods: 44 in-depth interviews, then participatory sessions combining Open Space, World Café and a fishbowl.

Core argument

A disruptive technology only works if end users take part in shaping it from the first stages, not just at the end.

Next Wave · Article 1 — Antonucci et al. (2025)

The authors frame agrivoltaics with Bower and Christensen's theory of disruptive innovation (1995): new technologies can transform a sector, but only if the people who must use them accept and understand them.

3 / 10

The technology and the gap

Solar panels and crops on the same land

What REGACE builds

Next-generation agrivoltaic greenhouses: photovoltaic panels combined with farming, plus CO2 enrichment for higher yields.

The promise: energy security and food security from the same land.

What research was missing

Most studies measure technical performance. Farmers' perceptions, concerns and needs were mostly ignored.

The authors call this a major barrier to adoption.

Next Wave · Article 1 — Antonucci et al. (2025)

Key sentence of the paper: success depends not only on technical efficacy but equally on social acceptability and contextual feasibility.

Who was chosen (purposive and snowball sampling)

Different farm types and sizes, different experience with technology, different regions, and different social, religious and ethnic backgrounds. Interviews in the farmers' own languages.

How they kept it rigorous

Triangulation: interviews cross-checked with sessions.
Member checking: early findings shown back to participants.

Interview topics: current farming practices, awareness of agrivoltaics, barriers and opportunities, social acceptability and farming identity, willingness to co-design, sustainability concerns. Interviews were recorded, transcribed and thematically analysed.

5 / 10

How a participatory session worked

From open agenda to structured dialogue

1 · INFORM

Scientific input

Technology partners explain the REGACE greenhouse.

2 · OPEN

Open Space

Farmers propose their own topics; breakout groups form around them.

3 · DEEPEN

World Café

Rotating tables answer targeted questions; hosts keep the thread; concept maps record ideas.

4 · RETURN

Fishbowl

Results are given back and discussed in a participatory restitution.

Why combine them? Open Space brings out themes freely; World Café refines them. Sessions repeat across the year to catch seasonal risks.

Next Wave · Article 1 — Antonucci et al. (2025)

The authors chose this mix for its capacity to promote egalitarian, inclusive and dynamic discussion. Note the order: participants set the agenda first, then go deeper.

7 / 10

Findings

Farmers say yes, but with conditions

Positive, if adapted

Openness to agrivoltaics with little difference between regions.

Acceptance depends on fitting local crops, climate and infrastructure.

Barriers

High upfront cost and uncertain return.

Complex maintenance; need for training, technical help and insurance.

Grid connection, licensing and bureaucracy.

Opportunities

Full-service contracts: due diligence, training, maintenance, updates.

Links with irrigation and precision farming.

Local energy communities to share benefits.

Without policy reform, adoption risks being limited to well-resourced farmers, widening inequality.

Next Wave · Article 1 — Antonucci et al. (2025)

Small and medium farms carry the biggest risk: they lack resources to manage the complexity. The authors call for streamlined regulation, easier grid connection and targeted financial incentives.

8 / 10

Our critical reading

Strong on listening, less clear on what changed

Strengths

Five countries and diverse farmers, not one "public".

Methods in sequence: each one feeds the next.

Results returned to participants (member checking, fishbowl).

Limits

Small sample, first year only; the authors call for large surveys.

Selection effect: farmers who join may already be positive.

Perception more than co-design: the paper doesn't yet show what changed in the greenhouse because of farmers.

Next Wave · Article 1 — Antonucci et al. (2025)

Link to our course: the professor's before/during/after test asks exactly the question the paper leaves open for later years: what changed in the technology because people participated?

9 / 10

What we take for our project

Lessons for our AI energy assistant

Borrow the sequence

Inform, Open Space, World Café, fishbowl: a tested path we can adapt for tenants.

Support, not just tech

Farmers asked for training and full service. Tenants will need the same: help, repairs, a person to call.

Watch inequality

Only well-resourced farmers may benefit. In our case: only digitally skilled tenants could.

Show the trace

We will document what tenants change in the assistant, the step this paper still leaves open.
