Social and digital vulnerabilities: the role of participatory processes in the reconfiguration of urban and digital space

Antonucci, Sorice & Volterrani (2022) · Frontiers in Political Science 4:970958

Presented by Next Wave

Mahsa Shahi · Kuhyar Saeedi · Farida Safdari · Gelila Tsehaye Melesse · Md Nazmul Islam · Pranto Argha Sarker

About 10 minutes. Authors from CNR-IRPPS (Antonucci), Luiss (Sorice) and Tor Vergata (Volterrani). A qualitative study of six Italian urban regeneration cases in peripheral, "liminal" areas.

2 / 10

The paper at a glance

Can participation include the most vulnerable, or only legitimise decisions?

Research question

How do organisations regenerating peripheral areas see their role, limits and chances inside the "neoliberal city"?

Where

Six cases in Rome, Naples, Milan, Turin, Florence and Reggio Calabria: two each in the North, Centre and South.

Method

Qualitative case studies: background research, then semi-structured interviews with each organisation (March–May 2022).

Core argument

Without real participation by people who live there, urban regeneration stays "on paper", and participation becomes a mere procedure.

Next Wave · Article 2 — Antonucci, Sorice & Volterrani (2022)

The paper's key contrast: in some cities, participation relies on digital tools (participatory budgets, consultations); in peripheries, it relies more on social infrastructure: civic committees, community foundations, collaboration agreements.

3 / 10

Key concept 1

The risk: participation as decoration

Neoliberal city

Efficiency, competition and "governance" replace political debate. Citizens become users of services, not makers of the agenda.

"Participationism"

Procedures that give access but no real power: they legitimise choices already made at the top.

Two side effects

The commons risk being privatised, and facilitators risk becoming a "technocracy" of participation.

The test they propose

Is inclusion just access, or is participation meaningful? Do citizens receive services, or help build the public agenda?

Next Wave · Article 2 — Antonucci, Sorice & Volterrani (2022)

The authors use seven components of democratic innovation (Smith 2009; Geissel and Joas 2013): inclusiveness, institutionalisation, popular control, social legitimation, transparency, efficiency and transferability, and question each one.

4 / 10

Key concept 2

Liminal spaces and digital capital

Liminal space

A space "at the boundary of two dominant spaces, which is not fully part of either" (Dale and Burrell, 2008): not clearly owned, shaped by marginalisation and gentrification.

When communities make it meaningful, it becomes a place of dwelling, not an empty edge.

Digital capital

Skills plus technology (Ragnedda). Like any capital, it accumulates unequally and reproduces inequality.

The most vulnerable have the least, and datafication and platforms widen the gap.

Next Wave · Article 2 — Antonucci, Sorice & Volterrani (2022)

Hybrid (physical plus digital) participatory processes in liminal spaces often start by chance: residents stumble into it by chance, through a discussion that meets a concrete interest. The paper links inclusive and meaningful participation (Geissel and Joas) as the condition for giving value to both on-site and online participation.

6 / 10

Findings

What the six cases show

Institutions: bureaucratic or absent

They rarely join bottom-up processes. All organisations say results would come faster if institutions took a non-bureaucratic and more directive approach.

Social capital is the engine

Committees, schools, associations and trusted networks open doors in places even the police could not enter.

Money shapes participation

Private funds bring an income logic; where third-sector resources lead, "creative participation" is stronger.

The pandemic went digital

Online events, digital communities and community media kept ties alive and created new formats.

Reggio Calabria quote, paraphrased: the area was so closed that not even the police could enter; the institutional answer was more police instead of social educators, which closed the community further. The third sector entered "on tiptoe" with a large network. The interviewee compares Arghillà to Scampia and Tor Bella Monaca.

8 / 10

Our critical reading

A sharp lens, but the residents' voice is missing

Strengths

Strong theory: separates access from real influence.

Italy-wide: North, Centre and South, very different actors.

Brings in digital inequality as part of vulnerability.

Limits

Only organisations interviewed: vulnerable residents speak through others.

Self-assessment: impacts are as reported by the organisations themselves.

Dense theory, lighter on practical design advice.

Next Wave · Article 2 — Antonucci, Sorice & Volterrani (2022)

An irony worth raising: a paper about the inclusion of vulnerable people relies on intermediaries, not on vulnerable people themselves. That is a methodological choice, but it mirrors the problem it describes.

What we take for our project

Tor Bella Monaca is in this paper

Our site is liminal

An interviewee compares Arghillà to Scampia and Tor Bella Monaca: hard to enter, closed by a security-only response.

Design for low digital capital

The AI assistant must work without an app too: paper, phone, a person.

Enter through social capital

Tenant committees, unions, charities and the parish: the "trusted bridges" of our Step 2 map.

Avoid participationism

Tenants must decide the fairness rule, not just approve one already written.

For discussion

Questions for the class

1

How can we tell real participation from "participationism" in a project we design ourselves?

2

Can digital tools build digital capital, or do they always favour those who already have it?

3

Should institutions lead participation in peripheries, or step back and support bottom-up groups?
