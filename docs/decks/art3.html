Hybrid Participation Innovation Lab · Article 3

The valuable plurality of the citizen sciences

van Oudheusden, Berti Suman, Huyse, Huyse & Medvecky (2024) · Science & Technology Studies 37(1) · Discussion paper

Presented by Next Wave

Mahsa Shahi · Kuhyar Saeedi · Farida Safdari · Gelila Tsehaye Melesse · Md Nazmul Islam · Pranto Argha Sarker

About 10 minutes. A short discussion paper, not an empirical study: the authors reflect on their experience as participant observers in contributory citizen science projects "in various parts of the world". Its three cases are in Belgium, Uganda and New Zealand.

2 / 10

The paper at a glance

Is "just collecting data" really second-class participation?

The common view

Most citizen science is contributory: citizens gather data for experts. Critics say this is not inclusive, transformative or democratic. Co-creation is presented as the ideal.

The authors' answer

Contributory projects can also be emancipatory: they can change policy, change behaviour and reach hard-to-reach groups. The future is a plurality of citizen sciences.

Next Wave · Article 3 — van Oudheusden et al. (2024)

The authors stress they are not against co-creation: all of them work on co-creative projects too. They are against the idea that co-creation is always the best form.

3 / 10

Three meanings of citizen science

From data gatherers to co-researchers

Contributory

Volunteers collect data; experts design and interpret. Tradition of amateur science (Bonney).

Participatory

Science opened up to wider publics and done by citizens themselves (Irwin, 1995).

Co-creative

Citizens take part in every stage, from the research question to the analysis (Shirk et al., 2012).

The critiques

Unpaid labour serving science or industry; experiential knowledge ignored; and in the US, participants are mostly white, above-median income, college-educated.

Next Wave · Article 3 — van Oudheusden et al. (2024)

Critiques cited: Mirowski 2017, Mahr and Dickel 2019, Gabrys 2017, Mahmoudi et al. 2022, Cooper et al. 2021. The EU, the OECD and national bodies now frame co-creation as the norm, which shapes funding.

The problem with ranking

The participation ladder makes a hierarchy

"best"

Non-participation

"worst"

Arnstein's ladder (1969), in simplified form

Why the authors push back

Treating co-creation as the top rung makes contributory projects look "less than", and this shapes expert judgement and funding.

But not everyone has the time, resources or commitment to co-create. And any participatory engagement raises questions of strategy and power.

This connects to our Step 4: the professor's five levels (inform to co-create) can be read as a ladder. The paper suggests choosing the level by purpose, not always aiming for the top.

5 / 10

Three contributory cases

Simple data collection, real impact

FLANDERS · 2018

CuriousNoses

20,000 people measured NO2 air pollution in their street for a month.

Impact: air quality became an election issue; a Brussels court accepted the data as indicative evidence; people changed how they commute.

UGANDA · 2019

ATRAP

25 trained volunteers reported snails that spread parasitic diseases, weekly, by phone app.

Impact: warning signs at risky sites; volunteers called "Snail Doctor"; one entered local politics; copied in Chad.

NEW ZEALAND · 2016–

Flip the Fleet

645 electric-car drivers share monthly data on distance, charging and efficiency.

Impact: better-informed buyers and a public debate on EV policy; it is slowly moving toward co-creation.

Next Wave · Article 3 — van Oudheusden et al. (2024)

CuriousNoses: court case Greenpeace v Flemish Region, October 2018. ATRAP: Action Towards Reducing Aquatic Snail-borne Parasitic Diseases, around Lake Albert, with feedback via WhatsApp and yearly refresher training. Flip the Fleet: car dealers reported customers bringing project charts to negotiations.

6 / 10

The core argument

Three ways to engage, all needed

Deliberation

Listening, questioning your own assumptions, learning together.

Participation

Mobilising like-minded people to have a voice in decisions.

Representation

Letting spokespersons and data speak on citizens' behalf in the political arena.

Low-threshold tools (cheap, simple, autonomous) let minorities and busy people join at scale, and scale makes institutions listen.

Next Wave · Article 3 — van Oudheusden et al. (2024)

Based on Mutz (2006) and Wesselink and Hoppe (2011). In CuriousNoses, validated protocols made the data credible to scientists and policymakers: it "could not be dismissed as irrelevant". That credibility came from working with research institutes and validating official models; the scale of participation added political weight. The paper also borrows "puzzling" and "powering" from Wesselink and Hoppe to say participation needs both dialogue and a voice.

7 / 10

A plea for pragmatism

Start from the problem, not the format

Ask first

1

What is the problem or challenge?

2

For whom, and why?

3

What change: scientific, societal or systemic?

Risks to manage

Powerful actors using volunteers' data "under the guise of opening and democratizing science".

Contribution turning into a one-way, "tick-box" consultation.

Citizens doing routinised labour for economic reasons.

Next Wave · Article 3 — van Oudheusden et al. (2024)

The authors' answer to these risks is to weave different approaches together rather than pick one mode, and to speak of "citizen sciences" in the plural.

8 / 10

Our critical reading

A useful correction, built on success stories

Strengths

Challenges a normative bias that shapes funding and evaluation.

Concrete impacts: a court case, elections, new local leaders.

Practical questions to choose the right format.

Limits

Authors' own experience as evidence, and only successful ones.

No data on who took part: did low thresholds really include minorities?

Impact depended on media and allies, not only on the format.

Next Wave · Article 3 — van Oudheusden et al. (2024)

In CuriousNoses, "thanks in large part to the news media, the campaign stimulated massive interest in air pollution". A contributory project without such allies might not have the same effect.

9 / 10

What we take for our project

A two-track citizen science design

Track 1 · wide

Every household contributes

A simple energy postcard or a smart plug: low effort, reaches busy and non-digital tenants, gives the scale that makes ATER and the City listen.

Track 2 · deep

Volunteers co-create

A smaller group designs the protocol, interprets the data with researchers and proposes changes to the AI and the fairness rule.

Plus: validated methods for credibility, and recognised "energy ambassadors", like the Snail Doctors.

Next Wave · Article 3 — van Oudheusden et al. (2024)

This is exactly how our Step 7 citizen science component is built. It also answers the professor's warning against an extractive model: track 2 keeps citizens in the interpretation.
