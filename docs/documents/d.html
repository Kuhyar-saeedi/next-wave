D · Citizen Science

Deliverable D

Deliverable D, Step 7 · Hybrid Participation Innovation Lab, Tor Vergata · Next Wave: Mahsa Shahi, Kuhyar Saeedi, Farida Safdari, Gelila Tsehaye Melesse, Md Nazmul Islam, Pranto Argha Sarker.

Tenants produce the knowledge the AI energy assistant is missing: how real households use energy, and where the forecasts fail them. Citizens take part not only in collecting data but in writing the protocol and interpreting the results, and their findings must receive an answer.

1. Research questions

Each question feeds a specific decision in the process.

Question Feeds
RQ1. When do households really use energy, and what stops them from using it in sunny hours? The design of the assistant's advice
RQ2. Where does the AI forecast go wrong, and for which kinds of households? The model and its household types
RQ3. How would each fairness rule change what real households receive? The fairness assembly (Gate 2)

The protocol is co-written by tenants and researchers in a workshop, then tested with five households to fix unclear steps before the full launch.

2. Roles: what citizens do

Citizens observe, measure, document, classify and interpret, on two tracks. Following van Oudheusden et al. (2024), even the contributory wide track keeps a door open to deeper roles.

Track Who Activities
Wide All households Document: a two-week energy postcard, ticking when big appliances run. Classify: "could I have moved this to midday? yes / no / why". Consent: optional sharing of 15-minute meter data.
Deep About 30 volunteer households Measure: smart plugs on the washing machine or water heater. Observe: photos of roof shade at fixed times. Interpret: label data, compare it with the AI forecast, propose changes.

Researchers train volunteers, run the data infrastructure and co-facilitate the data evenings. The technology provider receives findings and must answer them; it does not own the data.

3. Data, quality and uncertainty

The data must be credible enough for ATER, GSE and the provider to act on it.

  • Data collected: paper or app energy postcards, smart-plug readings, short diaries explaining why appliances ran when they did, roof-shade photos, and (with consent) 15-minute meter data.
  • Shared protocol: written by tenants and researchers together, piloted with five households.
  • Cross-checks: postcards and diaries are compared with meter data; mismatches are discussed, not deleted.
  • Training: a 30-minute session, a picture guide and an ambassador to call.
  • Honest uncertainty: every chart shows how sure we are, and small samples are labelled as such.

4. Interpretation and how knowledge enters decisions

Tenants and researchers read the results together at monthly data evenings (community room, with an online summary). Citizen knowledge then reaches a decision in five steps:

  1. Data: postcards, plug readings and photos are collected.
  2. Data evening: tenants and researchers interpret them together.
  3. Proposal: a written change request, with its evidence.
  4. Decision: the assembly or the provider must answer within a month.
  5. Change log: a public record of each request: accepted, changed, or refused with reasons.

Illustrative example: diaries show that shift workers cannot move laundry to noon. The assembly adds a "flexibility credit" for night-time water-heater timers, and the AI learns a new household type.

5. Ethics: tenants are not unpaid sensors

  • Data belongs to the CER. The provider only processes it, under the tenants' data charter (Gate 3).
  • Privacy by design. Aggregated by default, deleted after the pilot, GDPR consent asked for each use.
  • Opt out, no penalty. Not sharing data never reduces a household's share.
  • Right to challenge. Any finding can be contested at a data evening and re-checked.
  • Recognition. Named credit, certificates and a small allowance for volunteers.

6. Feedback to participants

  • Every household receives its own energy postcard: its usage pattern and what it could gain, in plain language.
  • Every month a "you said, we did" bulletin shows which findings changed the model, the messages or the fairness rule.
  • At the end of the pilot a courtyard event presents the results, and the public change log stays open for the monitoring group.

Reference

van Oudheusden, M., Berti Suman, A., Huyse, T., Huyse, H., and Medvecky, F. (2024). The valuable plurality of the citizen sciences. Science & Technology Studies, 37(1).
