The professor's seven questions

He will ask these during the presentation. Open each one to see our answer.

Who defined the problem?
At first the developers did. In Step 3 we rewrote it with the voices on our stakeholder map, and at Gate 1 tenants choose which issues are co-designed first. The listening phase confirms or corrects our assumptions.
Who is missing from your process?
Usually women carers, young people, shift workers, migrants, tenants without a contract and neighbours outside the community. Step 9 names how we reach each one: doorstep visits, mediators, evening and weekend slots, childcare, ambassadors and trusted third-sector groups.
What can participants really change?
The fairness rule, the data charter, whether the assistant advises or automates, its channels, and whether the pilot continues. These are four binding gates that developers sign in advance.
Why did you choose these methods?
Each performs a task: interviews explore, Open Space opens the agenda, the lottery assembly and fishbowl deliberate, World Café generates ideas, citizen science produces knowledge, the living lab tests. The Open Space, World Café, fishbowl sequence was tested in REGACE (Article 1).
What happens if stakeholders disagree with the developers?
The gate decision binds: the provider adapts or the pilot pauses. Disagreements are logged publicly, explored in a fishbowl and tested with data, and an external mediator is called before any override.
How does citizen-generated knowledge enter a decision?
Data, then a data evening where tenants and researchers interpret it, then a written proposal, then an answer from the assembly or provider within a month, recorded in a public change log.
What would count as evidence that participation worked?
At least one design element changed and is traced to tenants; the assembly mirrored the complex; vulnerable households were not worse off than under the default; tenants can explain the rule; a group keeps monitoring after the pilot.
